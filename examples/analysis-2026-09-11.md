# Analysis note: global news snapshot, 11 Sep 2026

_Author: Raul de la Hera_

> Example run of OSINT-AI over public RSS news. Public data only, and I've tried to stay honest
> about how much a single day of feeds can really tell you.

## 1. Scope and data

I collected 160 articles on 11 September 2026 from five public news RSS feeds: NYT World (55),
The Guardian World (45), Al Jazeera (25), BBC World (24) and NPR World (10). The pipeline ingests
the RSS, runs spaCy NER over it for people, organisations and places, builds an entity
co-occurrence graph, scores each article with VADER sentiment, geocodes the places through
Nominatim, and keeps a local retrieval layer on top for question-answering.

The one thing I'd flag before the rest: it's a single day, in English, from Western outlets. So
it shows what these five newsrooms picked that day. I read everything below as a picture of their
agenda and nothing more than that.

## 2. What the graph shows

The graph links two entities when they turn up in the same article, with no manual tagging. Three
clusters came out on top.

The heaviest by far is Russia and Ukraine. Russia-Ukraine is the single strongest link in the
corpus at 26 co-occurrences, with Europe-Ukraine (14) and Patriot-Ukraine (12) sitting behind it.
That Patriot link is the air-defence and weapons-supply story specifically, not the war in
general.

Next is a tight Middle East cluster built around Iran: Iran-US (13), Iran-Yemen (12), Iran-Red Sea
(9), with an Israel/Israeli sub-group hanging off it. That's the maritime-security and
regional-tension coverage.

Third is US domestic politics: Trump-US (11), JD Vance-US (10), Republicans-US (10).

One node ties the three together. "US" reaches into Ukraine aid, Iran and domestic politics, and
it comes out as the highest-centrality node in the graph. For a global news corpus that's the
obvious answer, which is part of why I trust the rest: if the US hadn't landed as the hub, I'd
assume something in the pipeline was broken.

## 3. Sentiment

Mean article sentiment on VADER's compound score is **−0.267**. Ninety-nine articles score
negative, 48 positive and 13 neutral, so about 62% read as negative.

Given how much of the day is conflict coverage, that fits. A couple of things keep me careful with
it. VADER scores the language of the coverage and not the events, and it only has short headline
and summary text to work from. I'd use it to watch the mood shift over time and lean on it very
little as an absolute figure.

## 4. Geography

The geocoded places pile up over Ukraine and Eastern Europe, the Gulf and the Red Sea, and
Washington. Same three stories as the graph, this time on a map. It earns its keep as a fast
"where is the attention today" view and I wouldn't ask more of it.

## 5. What I'd fix next

Entity resolution is the weak point and the first thing I'd change. "Israel", "Israeli" and
"Israelis" each get their own node. So do "UK" and "British", and "US" and "U.S.". That pads the
node count and splits one entity across several, so an alias and normalisation pass would pay off
the most.

NER also made a few plain mistakes. "AI" got tagged as a place (GPE), and a feed slug,
"anthony-albanese-september-11", slipped in as a person. A short clean-up with a stop-list clears
both.

The bigger caution is that co-occurrence isn't a relationship. Two entities sharing an article
only tells you they appeared together. It's a signal of where attention is pooling and a place for
a person to start digging, and nothing in here should be read as a proven link.

And the sourcing is narrow: five Western, English-language outlets. Adding non-Western and
non-English feeds would move the picture, and that's the honest way to cut some of the bias.

## 6. Takeaway

There's no single headline here and I don't think there should be. What the run shows is that
going from raw RSS to three coherent themes, a mood reading and a map took one automated pass. I'd
use it for triage: get the shape of the day's information fast, find the hubs and the bridges
between stories, then hand the threads worth chasing to a person who checks them against primary
sources.
